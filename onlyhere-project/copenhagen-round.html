Copenhagen Doorstep Round

Eleven stops in one loop. Six shops to pitch, seven Gemlyx entries that have never had a photograph taken of them, and one decision to make before you leave the flat.

Decide this before you go

Three deals, and they are not variations on one thing. They put the risk in different places, and walking in undecided is how you get let me think about it.

10% for Gemlyx usersEasiest yes, weakest deal

Costs them margin on people who would have walked in anyway. Costs you nothing, which is the problem: 10% off a bag of sweets is about five kroner, and nobody pays for a year of Gemlyx to save five kroner.

Their risk
Small but real, and it is theirs.
Your risk
None.
500 kr up front, free bags until it runs outLead with this

You hand them 500 kroner today. Subscribers show the offer, take a bag, the shop counts them down. When the money is gone, the offer ends. They cannot lose on this, and that is the entire pitch. It is not a discount you are asking them to fund. It is a sale.

Their risk
Zero, by construction. It is revenue.
Your risk
Exactly 500 kr per shop and it cannot exceed that. Ten bags is the honest number, and ten is a fixed ceiling rather than an exposure.
Why it sells
A free bag from an 1891 royal-warrant shop is a reason to subscribe you can photograph and tell someone. A 10% discount is not.
A proper write-up, no money either wayThe door, not the deal

For six of the stops below you have already done this and they do not know. For the other five it is the offer that gets you past the counter. Either way it is what you open with, because it changes the conversation from a salesman asking for a discount into someone who already recommended them asking for one thing back.

The first twenty seconds

Two versions. The one that matters is the first, and the shop is already on Gemlyx.

“Hej, jeg hedder Oliver. Jeg laver Gemlyx, en rejseguide til Danmark. I står faktisk allerede på den, se her. Har du to minutter?” Hi, I'm Oliver. I make Gemlyx, a travel guide to Denmark. You're already on it, look. Have you got two minutes?

And where they are not on it yet:

“Hej, jeg hedder Oliver, og jeg laver en rejseguide til Danmark. Jeg vil gerne skrive om jer — ordentligt, ikke bare en adresse. Og så vil jeg gerne spørge om én ting.” Hi, I'm Oliver, I make a travel guide to Denmark. I'd like to write about you properly, not just an address. And then I'd like to ask you one thing.

Have the page open before you walk in

Not the front page. Their page, already loaded, screen brightness up. The gap between “I have a website” and “here is what I have written about you” is the whole meeting.

What they will say

“What if people abuse it? Coming in again and again for a free bag?”

The one you predicted, and on the 500 kroner deal it has a clean answer: they cannot. You have prepaid a fixed number of bags. The shop counts them down and when they are gone the offer is over. Their exposure is zero whatever anybody does. Then, and only if they push further: the offer is visible only to people who have paid for a subscription, so the population that could try it is people who paid for the privilege. Do not lead with that second part. Offering to prevent abuse before they have raised it tells them there is an abuse problem.

“How many customers will this actually send me?”

You do not know, and you must say so. Gemlyx has no analytics at all — the tracker was removed on 23 August and the privacy policy says there is none. So: “I can't tell you, because I don't track visitors. That's exactly why I'm paying you up front instead of promising you traffic.” That sentence is worth more than any number you could invent, and it is the only version of it you can say honestly.

“Send me something and I'll look at it.”

The polite no. Take it, but not empty-handed: ask for the photograph before you go. It costs them nothing, almost nobody refuses, and it is the thing you actually came for. Then ask what year they opened and one thing about the shop nobody prints. You leave with the page improved whether or not the deal happens.

What to bring back

The deals may not happen. These four things cost the shop nothing, almost nobody refuses them, and every one of them fixes something that is currently wrong in the database.

  • A photograph. Sixty-two published entries have proper sources and no picture at all. Your camera is the only fix that exists for those.
  • The founding year, from them. Not from a website. That is a citable source and it is the field the pipeline keeps guessing at.
  • One true thing nobody prints. This is what the Gemlyx find field is for and it is the only part of an entry that cannot be researched from a desk.
  • Their opening hours, out loud, and whether their own website is right. Half the time it is not, and you will have heard it from the person who unlocks the door.

Never say

A number you do not measure

No visitor counts, no “we send X people a month”, no growth figures. There are no analytics on Gemlyx and your own privacy policy says so in writing. Inventing a number to a business partner is the one mistake here that would be difficult to walk back, and you do not need it: paying up front is the argument.

A date you cannot keep

Not “it'll be live next week”. The offer field is built and the paid plans are not switched on yet. Say what is true: the page exists today, the offer goes live when subscriptions do, and the 500 kroner is theirs either way.
